Behind the wall

What’s actually
behind the tiles.

A shower is a wall with eight things hidden in it. Pick one to see what it does — and what it costs when it fails quietly.

The only part you see

Tile over a waterproof backer board. The board is the part that matters: the tile is decoration, and a cracked grout line is just an early warning that water is already behind it.

Two valves, one room

Isolating valves on the hot and cold so this bathroom can be worked on without turning the whole house off. They seize when nobody touches them for years — ours get exercised at every visit.

The pipes themselves

Hot and cold stubs feeding the valve. Copper or PEX, running in the stud bay with a little slack so nothing is under tension. Every joint here is one we made, and it gets pressure-tested before the wall closes.

A 1.5 mm insurance policy

Steel plates over the notches where pipe sits close to the surface, so a tile screw or a picture hook can’t find it. Cheap, invisible, and the reason a lot of walls don’t get opened twice.

Where hot meets cold

The cartridge blends the two lines to the temperature you set and keeps it there when someone flushes downstairs. When it fails it usually shows up as no hot water on one side, or a shower that swings hot and cold.

The exit

The drain body and grate sit in the floor with a seal under them. Hair and soap bind to the grate before the trap; that is a five-minute job, not a reason to break the floor.

The water you never see move

A U-shaped bend that holds a plug of water, which is what stops sewer gas coming back into the room. Empty traps in unused bathrooms are the usual cause of “something smells”.

Why it glugs

The stack above lets air in behind the water going out. Without it the drain pulls against a vacuum, empties the trap as it goes and gurgles for hours. A blocked vent looks exactly like a blocked drain.

Follow the water

Four moves.
One drop of water.

  1. 01

    Find the main

    The street main runs under the ground outside. The curb stop is the valve that belongs to this house — we find it and prove it shuts before anything is opened.

  2. 02

    Pressure

    Through the foundation, past the meter and the regulator, which drops street pressure to something the house can hold. Split hoses and tired washers usually start here.

  3. 03

    Hot & cold

    Cold splits to the fixtures and fills the tank. Hot leaves the top of the tank and runs back along the wall to the same taps, on its own line.

  4. 04

    Drain

    Everything that came in leaves through a trap, down the stack and out to the sewer. The vent above keeps it moving instead of pulling a vacuum.

Straight answers

The questions
we get asked most.

Water is coming through the ceiling right now. What do I do?

Turn the water off at the main, then open a tap downstairs to take the pressure out of the pipes. Put a bucket under the drip and move anything electric out from underneath. Then call. Don’t put a ladder up into a wet ceiling on your own.

What does a repair cost?

The number comes from what failed, how much has to be opened to reach it, and what has to be put back afterwards. We look first, then give you a written itemised estimate. Nobody can price a leak honestly over the phone.

Will you have to cut my wall?

Sometimes, and only where it genuinely has to open. We tell you where before we start, and we leave it at a sandable finish rather than a hole with a rag in it.

Can I just shut the water off myself?

If you can find the main valve, yes: turn it clockwise until it stops and open a tap to drain the pressure. If it won’t turn, don’t force it — a seized valve that snaps is a much worse afternoon.

Copper or PEX?

Both work. Copper lasts, is repairable and doesn’t mind heat; PEX is quicker to run around corners and copes with a freeze that would split copper. We’ll tell you what suits the house rather than what happens to be on the van.

Do you work on old houses?

Yes — galvanised, lead joints, no access and forty years of other people’s repairs. We’ll tell you what is safe to leave alone and what is worth replacing while the wall is already open.

Are you licensed and insured?

Ask for the licence number and the insurance certificate when we quote, and we’ll email both before any work is scheduled. It should be the first thing you ask any plumber, including us.
